01 · The field

Arriving late to the parlour

In brief

A receiver never starts anything. They join an argument that began years before, about a loan, a property and the people in it, and they leave before it ends. This section sets out that picture, Kenneth Burke’s ‘parlour’, and explains why the rest of the treatise starts from conversations rather than from a plan.

Kenneth Burke’s most quoted passage, from The Philosophy of Literary Form (1941), describes arriving late at a parlour where a heated discussion is already under way. Nobody stops to tell you what it is about; the discussion began long before you, and nobody present could retrace all its steps. You listen for a while until you think you have caught its drift, and then you put in your oar. Someone answers, someone defends you, someone takes the other side. The hour grows late, you have to leave, and the discussion carries on without you.

Chris Mowles uses that passage to open “Organising as continuous conversation” (August 2025), and applies it to anyone who joins an organisation: the consultant, the new employee, the newly appointed manager. You pitch into an argument with several threads, without the history you would need to follow it. Once you are in, he argues, not participating is as significant as participating, because people have already noticed you; and the moment you speak your truth you have taken a side in the organisation’s politics. There is no safe space, and no outside vantage point from which to observe without influencing.

Why the parlour matters

It helps to take Burke’s image literally for a moment. Imagine walking into a room where people are arguing about something important to them. You do not know when it started, who first said what, or which grudges sit behind the politest remarks. If you speak at once, you will probably answer a question nobody is asking. If you wait for someone to brief you, you will wait for ever, because nobody in the room could give a complete account even if they wanted to. What you can do is listen, catch the drift, and then join in carefully, knowing that whatever you say will be heard against everything that has been said before. Mowles’s point is that organisations work like this all the time, and that newcomers are not the exception but the rule.

No figure in property finance fits Burke’s parlour better than a fixed charge receiver. The receiver arrives by deed, usually on a Friday, into a conversation that began when the loan was written: between the lender and the borrower, the borrower and the tenants, the managing agent and the contractors, the tenants and each other.

The receiver is formally the agent of the mortgagor under the charge and the Law of Property Act 1925, and practically the newest person in every room. Every letter is read before it is understood. Every silence is interpreted. The appointment is not a thing the receiver picks up and manages; it is a conversation the receiver joins, changes and is changed by, and leaves before it ends.

What this means for a receivership

A receivership makes the parlour unusually vivid, because the receiver arrives with legal authority but almost no history. The lender knows why the loan was written and why it went wrong, or thinks it does. The borrower has a story about the bank. The tenants have their own account of the landlord, the agent and each other. None of these accounts is simply true or false; each is a position in a long argument. The receiver’s first letters, visits and decisions will be read as moves in that argument, whether the receiver intends them that way or not. That is why this treatise asks receivers to begin by finding out what conversation they have joined.

This treatise is the seventh in the receivership series, and it does something the sixth did not. The Cynefin Field Kit gave receivers twenty-seven instruments for seeing an appointment: domain models, an estuarine map, probe portfolios, a triad. Mowles’s essay “Complexity and inside-out thinking” (February 2025), on which this treatise is built, names exactly that kind of instrument (two-by-two grids, parts-and-whole charts, “even imagined estuaries”) as the outside-in thinking his method sets out to reverse. So this treatise turns the kit inside out. It starts not from a map of the appointment but from what happens between people in it, in the present, and asks what those local patterns are a case of.

It draws on the whole of Mowles’s Substack, On complexity, as far as it could be read from here: twenty-five posts read in full, two in part, and six more known only by their titles, every one listed in the corpus with what was taken from it and where it is used. Behind those posts stand the four traditions of complex responsive processes that Mowles has long described: the complexity sciences, Norbert Elias’s process sociology, the pragmatism of Mead and Dewey, and the group analysis of S. H. Foulkes. The receivership readings, the instruments and any errors are this treatise’s own.

Why this matters for a receiver

For a receiver, the practical lesson is modest and immediate: before deciding anything substantial, find out what the people involved have been arguing about, and assume that your first moves will be read as taking a side. Everything else in the treatise builds on that.

02 · The instruments

Twenty-two instruments, and how to use them

An inside-out treatise with instruments in it is a small paradox, and it is worth being honest about. Every diagram here is a map of some kind. The instruments are not meant to replace the conversation; they are meant to rehearse it, to slow down a moment that in practice lasts seconds (a tenant’s question on the forecourt, a phone call before the credit committee) so that it can be noticed. Most of them ask you to choose, and then show you how your choice was read. None of them gives an answer you could deliver.

03 · Start with experience

Start with experience: play the appointment

In brief

Before any theory, you can play a whole appointment. The simulation lets you make thirteen decisions over a hundred days and watch their consequences unfold, some of them weeks later. The rest of the treatise then explains why it turned out as it did.

Mowles’s essay “Starting with experience” (April 2026) gives this treatise its order of work. Inquiry, he argues, should begin with what is happening between people now, including how they make sense of it, and move outwards to the general; ends and means are entangled, and understanding deepens as the work proceeds. So before the theory, the appointment.

Why start with play

Most professional texts begin with principles and end with examples. Mowles argues for the reverse: begin with experience, notice what happened and how people made sense of it, and only then reach for general ideas. The simulation is a way of giving every reader the same experience to reflect on. It is not a test with right answers. It is a shared case, so that when later sections talk about promises coming back, stories spreading or uncertainty being pushed down, you will have seen it happen on a parade you have just managed.

The instrument below plays the composite parade used throughout the treatise for a hundred days, through thirteen decisions taken from the casebook: the appointment call, the letters, the director’s post, the café, the stair light, the valuation, the first case meeting, the arrears, the committee’s question about control, the fee and the KPI, a small edit to a report, the café’s surrender, and an investor’s call. Each decision has three options. None is labelled. Each moves ten relationships on the map and six gauges on the right, and some choices are remembered: they return weeks later, as promises, valuations and favours do.

How the simulation remembers

The most important feature of the simulation is memory. Some decisions do not show their full effect straight away. A timetable promised on day one is a relief to everyone on day one; it becomes a problem on day thirty-four, when it has slipped and the committee remembers who set it. A valuation accepted without question looks like good news; it becomes bad news when a buyer’s surveyor reads the leases. Real appointments work like this. The textbook move is often the one that relieves anxiety now and defers its cost, and the inside-out move is often the one that costs something now and pays later.

Play it once as you would actually act. Then run the presets and compare them in the history. The pattern that usually emerges is the one Mowles’s posts would predict. The outside-in, textbook choices win the early weeks on lender confidence, because they relieve anxiety, and lose them later, when the timetable slips, the valuation turns out to rest on an untested assumption and the established story about the receiver hardens.

The inside-out choices cost confidence early and recover it as promises hold. The simulation is a model, and a model is a map; the weights are this treatise’s judgement, offered to be argued with. Its purpose is not to score you but to give the rest of the treatise something to explain.

Why this matters for a receiver

Playing the appointment once honestly, and then comparing the presets, shows a pattern that matters in practice: the moves that make the first month feel safest are often the ones that make the fourth month hardest. A receiver who knows this can explain to a lender why a slower start may be the safer course.

04 · Two stances

Outside-in and inside-out

In brief

There are two ways of looking at an appointment. Outside-in starts from a picture of the whole, such as an organisation chart or a workout template, and fits people into it. Inside-out starts from how people are actually relating and talking, and builds understanding from there. Both have their uses; this section explains why Mowles thinks the second is usually neglected.

Most of the thinking offered to organisations, Mowles writes, consists of maps and frameworks of a world assumed to exist “out there”. Two-by-two grids, parts-and-whole charts and frameworks are realist and representationalist: they assume the territory can be mapped before the journey begins, and they make implicit sense to bodies that find their way through space. That is outside-in thinking. It begins with a whole and works down to its parts.

An everyday illustration

Think of two ways to understand a family. One is to draw a family tree: who is married to whom, who are the children, who lives where. It is accurate and useful, and it tells you almost nothing about why Christmas is difficult. The other is to sit at the table and listen: who talks over whom, which subjects are avoided, who is everyone’s confidant. The tree is outside-in; the table is inside-out. Lenders and receivers mostly work with trees: facility agreements, tenancy schedules, rent rolls. These are necessary. But the decisions that shape an appointment are made at the table.

Receivership is saturated with it. The appointment arrives as a set of parts within a whole: a lender with a credit committee and a relationship manager, a borrower to be managed and then excluded, an agent with a mandate, a rent roll, a schedule of arrears, a valuation on a stated basis, an exit strategy chosen from a short list of boxes. The lender’s report template is an outside-in instrument; so is the receiver’s own appointment checklist. None of this is wrong, and much of it is required. The difficulty comes when it is mistaken for the appointment itself.

The alternative Mowles proposes is inside-out: an inquiry that starts with the patterns of relating between people in the present, including how they make sense of those patterns, and moves from the particular to the general. In “Starting with experience” (April 2026) he puts the core assumption plainly: what is “out there” is also “in here”. Language fails at this point, he admits, because there is no clean inside or outside; the local and the global are completely intertwined. The lender’s culture is not somewhere above the appointment. It is present in the first phone call with the relationship manager, in what she can and cannot say before Thursday’s committee.

Why ‘in here’ matters

When Mowles says that what is ‘out there’ is also ‘in here’, he means something quite practical. We tend to talk about ‘the market’, ‘the lender’s culture’ or ‘the regulator’ as if they were forces somewhere above us. But we only ever meet them in particular conversations: a relationship manager hesitating on a phone call because of an internal review, an investor quoting a yield he heard at lunch, a valuer cautious because of a recent claim. The large patterns are present in these small moments. That is why an inside-out receiver pays attention to small moments: they are where the large patterns can actually be seen and, sometimes, changed.

In “The problem with lenses” (September 2026) Mowles takes the point further, against the habit of examining things “through the lens of” a theory. A lens implies a detached observer choosing among predefined views of an object that already exists. There is no pre-existing organisation waiting to be seen, he argues, and no lens; knower and known are made in the inquiry. For a receiver that is not a philosophical nicety. The receiver is not looking at the appointment. The receiver is in it, and from the first letter is one of the people who make it what it is.

The instrument draws one parade appointment both ways: first as an organisation chart of eleven parties, then, turned inside out, as the same eleven people caught up in ten conversations that the chart leaves out, from “Why did we approve this in 2019?” to “They’re vultures; the parade will go to auction.”

Why this matters for a receiver

A receiver does not have to choose one stance. The lender needs the outside-in map, and the law and the reporting depend on it. The point is to notice when the map is doing the thinking, and to go and listen at the table before deciding what the map means.

05 · The map

Regulative ideas, and the map that keeps growing

In brief

When frameworks meet messy situations, they rarely get thrown away; they get more elaborate. This section explains Mowles’s argument that adding detail to a map does not necessarily bring you closer to what is happening, and can be a way of avoiding it.

Mowles borrows from Kant the term regulative ideas: ways of thinking that guide inquiry and can be applied across circumstances without ever being fully achieved. The idea of a whole made of parts is one. Kant meant such ideas to be held as heuristics, not mistaken for features of the world. Mowles’s complaint is that in contemporary organisations parts-and-whole thinking is used without that reflection, as if the whole really existed and the task were simply to manage its parts.

What a regulative idea is

Kant’s distinction is easier than it sounds. Some ideas describe things we can check: the number of units on a parade, the rent on a lease. Others guide how we think without ever being fully checkable: the idea that the parade is a single ‘asset’, or that a portfolio is a ‘whole’ made of ‘parts’. These guiding ideas are useful. Problems start when we forget they are guides and treat them as facts, so that we start saying ‘the asset needs’ or ‘the portfolio requires’ as if the asset could need anything.

He describes what happens when these models meet complexity. They do not give way; they grow. The whole is said to be open to its environment. Then it becomes a series of nested, incomplete wholes, each open to the next. Then the relationships between the parts are made non-linear, with negative and positive feedback. Then, because the parts are self-interpreting, “rationally-irrational human beings”, mechanistic models shade into critical ones that take argument and meaning into account. Each step makes the model more elaborate. None stops it being applied, as Mowles puts it, in a logical manner. In “Starting with experience” he adds the observation that every practitioner will recognise: the seeds of future difficulties are contained in today’s solutions.

A lender’s workout process often follows this exact path. It begins with a strategy grid: hold or sell, tenanted or vacant. It acquires a boundary (the appointment), arrows in and out (the market, the council), nesting (the lender’s book, the sector), sensitivity analyses for non-linear effects, and finally a stakeholder-management section for the people who answer back. By the time the paper reaches the committee it is a remarkable document. The parade, meanwhile, has moved on.

Recognising elaboration in practice

There is a simple test for whether a strategy paper is elaborating rather than understanding. Ask whether each new section was written after talking to someone on the property, or after thinking harder about the previous section. Elaboration grows from the page; understanding grows from contact. A paper that has acquired a risk register, a stakeholder map and three sensitivity tables, but no new conversation, has probably moved further from the parade, not closer.

Step through the elaboration below. The blue dots are what is actually happening on the parade; the brass lines are the map. Watch the two meters on the right. The point is not that maps are useless (receivers need them, and lenders require them) but that elaborating a map is not the same as getting closer to what is happening, and can be a way of avoiding it.

Why this matters for a receiver

Receivers are often asked for more detailed plans. The lesson here is not to refuse but to notice the difference between a plan that has been made more detailed and one that has been made more accurate, and to spend time on the second.

06 · The parlour

Organising as continuous conversation

In brief

If an appointment is a conversation, then every move a receiver makes, including saying nothing, is a contribution to it. This section explains why there is no neutral position, and why attention matters more than authority.

If organising is conversation, then knowledge in an organisation is, in Mowles’s pragmatist account, produced by people talking, arguing, forming and resisting alliances and clarifying what they mean. It is fallible and “good enough for now”, and when circumstances change people have to think differently, and their understanding of themselves and of the debate shifts as they do. Organising has no start and no end; it happens across what he calls a “group of groups”, and it is often heated, because people’s valuations matter to them. Commitments become clear only when they are said aloud and meet difference.

Two practical consequences follow for a receiver. The first is that there is no neutral move. Speaking takes a side; asking takes a side, because a question tells everyone what you are curious about; silence takes a side, because it lets others say what your silence means. The second is that the receiver’s main resource is not authority but attention: the capacity to notice, in Mowles’s phrase, recurring patterns in a discussion, which may open new options for a while, until the patterns shift again.

Three kinds of move

It can help to think of a receiver’s options in any conversation as three kinds of move. Speaking states a view; it is clear, and it takes a side. Asking signals curiosity; it keeps more relationships open, but it also tells everyone what you think is important. Staying silent seems safest, but others will decide what your silence means, and they will usually decide the worst. The instrument lets you try each, and the results are instructive: silence scores worst, and asking first and speaking later scores best, which is close to what Burke describes: listen, catch the drift, then put in your oar.

His earlier essay “Stability and change in the minute particulars” (April 2026) describes joining Ralph Stacey’s research group, four years into its life, as exactly this experience, and quotes Richard Bernstein quoting Burke. It also names the pragmatic insights about group life that the parlour makes vivid: the group precedes and outlasts the individual; contributions are reinterpreted, repackaged or dismissed by others; an idea can lie dormant for half an hour and be seized on again; and silence and speech both shape what happens.

What ‘good enough for now’ means

When Mowles calls knowledge in organisations ‘good enough for now’, he is not being casual about truth. He means that what a group understands at any moment is the product of its conversations so far, tested against what has happened, and open to revision when something new happens. For a receiver this is a relief rather than a weakness. It means a strategy note at week three does not have to be right for ever. It has to be the best shared understanding available at week three, written so that people can see when it needs to change.

The instrument puts you in the parlour for six weeks. At six moments (the café owner on the forecourt, the relationship manager before the committee, the agent asking to keep the instruction, the director at the tenants’ meeting, a valuation that lands conveniently near the loan, a resident’s daughter about the stair light) you can speak, ask, or stay silent. Each choice is read, and the reading moves your standing with each party. Try each preset, then compare them in the history.

Why this matters for a receiver

In practice: decide what your silence will be read as before you stay silent; prefer questions in the first weeks; and when you do speak, say which side your view takes and why.

07 · Gesture

Gesture and response: the notice to tenants

In brief

A message does not mean what its sender intends; it means what it calls out in the people who receive it. This section uses the receiver’s first letter to tenants to show how one gesture can mean twelve different things, and why receivers should plan sequences rather than single messages.

The pragmatist root of complex responsive processes is George Herbert Mead’s account of communication as a social act: a gesture by one person calls out a response from another, and the meaning of the gesture lies in the whole act, gesture and response together, not in the intention of the one who made it. Stacey built his theory of organising on this; Mowles has written about it at length, and his Substack carries a post titled “On the limits to ‘sending out a clear message’” (August 2025) with the subtitle “Gesture and response”. That post could not be read from here, so this section rests on the tradition rather than on its text.

Gesture and response, simply put

George Herbert Mead’s idea is easiest to grasp with a handshake. If I hold out my hand, the meaning of that gesture is completed only when you respond: by shaking it, by ignoring it, by stepping back. My intention matters, but it does not settle the meaning. The same is true of letters, emails and announcements. A receiver may draft a notice with great care for clarity, and the notice will still be read through each recipient’s history, fears and relationships. Clarity of drafting is necessary; it is not sufficient.

A receiver’s first gesture to the occupiers of a property is the clearest case in the profession. The statutory letter is drafted to be clear: who has been appointed, under what powers, where rent is now to be paid, whom to contact. It is the same letter for everyone. But it is not received as the same letter. For the pharmacist’s head office it is routine correspondence.

For the café owner, who has been told for weeks by the director that receivers are vultures, it is confirmation. For an eighty-one-year-old resident it is frightening, and her daughter will ring the agent. For the tenant whose flat is sublet it is something to hide. Its meaning is distributed across those responses; the receiver did not send one message but twelve.

Why the same letter lands differently

It is worth pausing on why the statutory letter produces such different responses. The pharmacy reads it as routine because its head office deals with receiverships regularly. The café owner reads it through weeks of the director’s warnings. The older resident reads it through fear of losing her home, and her daughter through anger on her mother’s behalf. The subtenant reads it as danger, because his position is not what the lease says. Nothing in the letter changes between these readings. What changes is the person, and what they already believe.

“Stability and change in the minute particulars” adds the second half of the insight: small gestures can destabilise what is taken for granted and escalate into much wider change. The stair light fixed on the day it is reported does more for an appointment than a well-drafted letter, because of how it is read and retold. Mowles’s resources post “Conveying complex experience” (August 2026) points the same way, quoting Peirce’s instruction to begin with people “and their conversation” rather than with pure ideas.

Choose one of four first gestures below and watch twelve people on the parade make it mean something. Then compare all four. No gesture is warm for everyone, which is the practical lesson: plan a sequence of gestures for the people the first one will miss.

Why this matters for a receiver

The practical consequence is to plan a first week, not a first letter: the statutory notice for everyone, then visits for those it will frighten or miss, then a channel the tenants already use, and only then the rent demand.

08 · Levers

Levers, figurations, and forming and being formed

In brief

We often talk about change as pulling levers. This section explains when that image works (for concrete systems like rates and platforms) and why it fails for people, whose responses to any lever depend on their relationships with everyone else involved.

In “Pulling levers for organisational change” (February 2026) Mowles starts from a Prime Minister’s complaint to a parliamentary committee that every time he went to pull a lever, a whole bunch of regulations got in the way. What interests Mowles is not regulation but the image of the lever, and the assumption that comes with it: that change is something done to an organisation from outside, by someone in control. Levers work, he allows, for concrete systems such as a tax rate or an IT platform, where a small adjustment can have large and predictable effects. They work badly for culture, mindset and values, which are webs of shared meaning built up in interaction and cannot be reliably steered.

His examples are telling. The NHS Choose and Book system, introduced in 2004 on the assumption that patients would behave like consumers choosing providers from performance data, was quietly dropped in 2015 after costing over £356 million; its evaluators found that staff resistance reflected professional values, relationships and the identity of the NHS as a universal service, not mere obstruction. And Sue Dopson’s study of treatment for glue ear, published in Organization Studies in 2005, found that guidelines and targets based on strong evidence still struggled to change practice, which she explained with Norbert Elias’s figurational sociology: many interdependent groups meant that no single group, the Department of Health included, could control the outcome.

What a figuration is, slowly

Norbert Elias used the word figuration to replace two misleading pictures. One is the picture of separate individuals making free choices. The other is the picture of ‘society’ as a thing above individuals, pushing them around. Elias asked us instead to picture people bound together by needing each other, like dancers in a dance or players in a game, where each move changes what the others can do next. Power, in this picture, is not something one person owns; it is the balance of how much each needs the other. Even the strongest party is constrained by those who depend on it.

Elias’s word for such a web is figuration: people bonded by interdependence, whose actions interlock so that outcomes emerge that nobody intended, not even the most powerful. In a figuration small interventions can produce large effects and large ones none. Unintended outcomes, Mowles stresses, are not necessarily evidence of weak leadership; they are what interdependence produces. A receivership is a figuration in miniature. Raising the shop rents at review “pulls a lever” on the rent roll; it also tells the café owner that the director was right, moves the WhatsApp group, gives the buyers a story about voids, and may leave the receiver with two surrenders and a lower value than before.

Why unintended outcomes are normal

If an appointment is a figuration, then unintended outcomes are not mistakes to be eliminated but the ordinary result of many people responding to each other. A receiver who raises rents intends a higher rent roll. The café owner, the director, the gossip, the buyers and the valuer all respond in their own ways, and the net result is something nobody chose. Mowles is careful here: this does not mean leaders are powerless or blameless. It means that good practice involves anticipating responses, testing moves in small ways first, and watching closely what happens.

The figuration also explains Mowles’s most persistent theme, the social paradox of forming and being formed. “Resources on the complexity of social life” (August 2026) sets it against individualist and structuralist accounts: people form society while being formed by it, simultaneously; individuality is achieved socially; there is, with Elias, a “society of individuals” in which I and we are inseparable. “What will become of us?” (March 2026) adds that this is why argument rarely changes minds where identity is at stake, and why relationships do. 09 · We

The established and the outsiders: who is “we”?

In brief

Groups that have been together a long time tend to protect themselves with stories about who belongs and who does not. A receiver arrives as the newest outsider, and the established story about them arrives first. This section explains how that works and what a receiver can do about it.

“Who are we, and what do we think we are doing?” (July 2026) is Mowles’s most direct essay on the first-person plural. Belonging to a “we” is a human need and a source of strength; it is also, he argues, a temptation and a danger. Groups that feel threatened praise themselves into what Elias called a heroic “we”, defined against a denigrated “them”, and the more cohesive the group, the less it notices its own part in what happens.

He is sharp about management practices that manufacture we-ness on purpose (he discusses Russell Ackoff’s idealised design as having something cult-like about it) and he distinguishes, after the anthropologist Didier Fassin, between resentment, which answers a real wrong, and ressentiment, a lasting posture of grievance. What he offers instead of enforced positivity is hope: a disposition that stays with difficulty rather than talking it away.

The study behind the idea

Elias and Scotson’s study is worth knowing in outline. In a small English town, an older neighbourhood and a newer one were alike in almost every measurable way: the same kinds of work, similar incomes, similar housing. Yet the older residents looked down on the newcomers, kept them out of local clubs and spread stories about their supposed rough habits. The researchers concluded that the difference was not money but cohesion. The established residents knew each other, shared a history and could act together; the newcomers could not. Their stories about the newcomers were powerful because they were told and retold among people who trusted each other.

The study behind the heroic “we” is Norbert Elias and John Scotson’s The Established and the Outsiders, which Mowles also draws on in his essay on gossip. In a small English community, two groups of working people, alike in class, income and occupation, lived side by side. The established group had been there longer and was more tightly knit; through gossip it praised itself and stigmatised the newcomers, who were too loosely connected to answer. Power, Elias concluded, followed cohesion rather than wealth.

A parade in receivership is often exactly that figuration. The long-standing occupiers (the café owner, the launderette, the barber, the older residents, the director who owned the parade for years) know each other and share a history, and usually a grievance. The newer occupiers (the nail bar, the nurses who share a flat, the charity shop with a changing manager, a national pharmacy) are loosely connected and have little standing. The receiver is the newest outsider of all. The established story about the receiver (in this composite, the director’s line that receivers are vultures and the parade will go to auction) is in circulation before the receiver has written a letter.

Grievance and posture

Didier Fassin’s distinction, as Mowles uses it, is helpful when dealing with an angry borrower. Resentment is a response to something that really happened: a broken promise, an unfair decision. It can be answered. Ressentiment is a settled posture of grievance that has become part of who someone is, and it tends to absorb every new event as further proof. Most real anger mixes the two. A receiver who treats all of it as posture usually makes it worse. Hearing the real core, even without agreeing to anything, can take the heat out of the rest.

“What will become of us?” (March 2026) explains why that story is not changed by argument. Where identity is at stake, being persuaded feels like being diminished; people change their minds through relationships, slowly, not through being shown they are wrong. For a receiver the practical consequence is to find the hub of the established group and the small gestures that its members will retell, and to take the director’s grievance seriously before deciding it is a posture. Raise the cohesion slider and the established group becomes harder to reach from outside, and easier to move from within.

Why this matters for a receiver

On a new appointment, find the hub of the established group early, take the borrower’s grievance seriously before deciding it is a posture, and remember that warmth among the newer occupiers rarely reaches the older ones on its own.

10 · Local and global

The local and the global: what is this a case of?

In brief

Small events on a property can be evidence about much larger patterns. This section explains the question Mowles gives his research students, ‘what is this a case of?’, and how a receiver can use it to read a site meeting as information about a lender, or a market.

The inside-out method moves from the particular to the general, and treats local patterns as fractal: what happens between people here reproduces, at its own scale, what happens more widely. Mowles is careful about this. In “The practice of complexity” (June 2026), reporting the year’s Complexity and Management Conference, he describes his own position as one “perspective of perspectives” among others, aiming to honour the idea that the social world is complex all the way down, and not claiming a monopoly on the truth.

The working question he gives his doctoral students comes from C. Wright Mills’s The Sociological Imagination: what is your experience a case of? Asked of a receivership, it is a powerful question. The agent blaming the contractor for the stair light, and the contractor blaming the agent’s purchase order, is a case of something: of blame moving downwards.

So, it turns out, is the tenants’ blaming the receiver for a rent rise the director agreed, the lender’s panel review blaming receivers’ fees, the credit committee blaming the originators who have left, and the market blaming “valuation lag” after every downturn. Whether it is the same pattern at each scale is a matter for argument, not proof. Asking the question is how a site meeting becomes evidence about a market.

How to ask the question well

‘What is this a case of?’ is a deceptively simple question. Asked well, it moves in two steps. First, describe the event carefully, in the terms of the people involved: the agent said the contractor was late; the contractor said the purchase order was too small. Then ask what more general pattern this might be one example of: perhaps blame moving down to whoever has least power to refuse it. Finally, look for the same pattern elsewhere and see whether it holds. The aim is not proof but a better question to take into the next conversation.

“Conveying complex experience” recommends Svend Brinkmann’s Qualitative Inquiry in Everyday Life on the same paradox: writing about the mundane means saying what it is a case of and why it matters. For a receiver, the mundane is the material: the file note, the tenant’s email, the joke in the case meeting. Choose one of four patterns below and zoom out from the site meeting to the property credit market.

Why this is not mere anecdote

Critics sometimes call this kind of reasoning anecdotal. The difference is in the discipline. An anecdote is a story told to illustrate a point already decided. Asking what a story is a case of starts from the story and is open about the pattern; it then looks for other cases that might confirm or contradict it. Done with others, and repeated across appointments, it becomes a form of evidence: partial and provisional, but grounded in what actually happened.

Why this matters for a receiver

Keep a short note of each surprise on an appointment and the pattern you think it is a case of. Over several appointments, the notes become a store of practical knowledge about lenders and markets that no report contains.

11 · Three dilemmas

Three dilemmas of the inside-out method

In brief

Mowles is candid that starting from local experience raises three hard questions. Can small groups say anything about the wider world? Can what they find be generalised? And do they simply repeat what they already believe? This section explains each dilemma and his answer to it.

Mowles does not present inside-out thinking as unproblematic. In “Complexity and inside-out thinking” he sets out three dilemmas, and “Starting with experience” returns to them. They are worth taking one by one, because each is a question a sceptical lender would ask of a receiver who worked this way.

Can the local say anything about the global?

Only if people are understood as thoroughly social selves, shaped by society and shaping it through continuous interaction. If that is so, then examining how people relate and how they have been socialised reveals wider social patterns, because those patterns are in them. Mowles adds a caution: individuals alone are limited by their own biases and habits of thought, which is why the method works through groups.

The first dilemma in plain terms

The first worry is that a group of people talking about their own experience can only ever describe themselves. Mowles’s answer depends on a particular view of what a person is. If each of us is shaped through and through by the society we grew up in, then our habits, assumptions and ways of speaking carry that society inside them. Studying how a group behaves is therefore also studying, in miniature, the wider society that formed them. The caution is that one person alone sees mostly their own habits, which is why the method works through groups.

Is it generalisable?

A group might only describe its own circumstances. Mowles answers with S. H. Foulkes, the founder of group analysis: socialisation is always imperfect, and any group of more than eight people will contain enough deviation from social norms to say something about general trends. Understanding produced this way is manifold, partial and transient. His image for its strength is C. S. Peirce’s.

In “Some Consequences of Four Incapacities” (1868), Peirce argued that reasoning should not form a chain no stronger than its weakest link, but a cable whose fibres may be ever so slender, provided they are numerous and intimately connected. That is the standard: good enough to act on, strengthened by reading other accounts of the same phenomena and by keeping the inquiry going.

Is it over-determined?

If society shapes how we think, an inquiry may only reproduce existing power relations and what is taken for granted. Mowles’s answer is free association, as practised in group analysis: no fixed agenda and no required outcome, so that participants can surprise themselves and unconscious, hidden social processes come into view. He also turns to Jean Piaget’s phrase “liberated convictions”, from the clinical method set out in The Child’s Conception of the World (1929): knowledge produced by questioning and probing, which accepts the shaping power of existing understanding but still yields insights that are the participant’s own. This is the social paradox of forming and being formed, applied to knowledge itself.

Why a cable, not a chain

Peirce’s image deserves a moment. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link; if one assumption fails, the whole argument fails. Much professional reasoning is built like this: comparable sales, then a yield, then an assumption about possession, then a value. A cable is made of many thin fibres twisted together. Any single fibre may be weak, but because there are many, and they come from different places, they do not all fail at once. Mowles suggests that knowledge built from many partial accounts, gathered from different people, is strong in this second way.

The next two instruments take the second and third dilemmas in turn: a cable you build from many accounts and load-test against a chain of expert reasoning; and a case meeting run twice, once to an agenda and once with none.

What ‘liberated convictions’ means

Piaget’s phrase comes from his work questioning children about how they understood the world. He found that some answers were simply repeated from adults, some were made up to please the questioner, and some were genuinely the child’s own: shaped by what they already knew, but worked out freshly in response to good questions. Mowles borrows the idea to answer the worry that people can only repeat what they have been taught. With open questioning and enough room, people can arrive at insights that are genuinely their own.

Why this matters for a receiver

Each dilemma has a receivership equivalent: is my reading of this parade only my own? Would it hold on another appointment? Am I just confirming what the lender already thinks? The instruments that follow, the cable and the meeting without an agenda, are practical ways of answering them.

12 · Paradox

Six paradoxes a receiver cannot resolve

In brief

Some tensions in receivership cannot be resolved, only held: being in charge without being in control, being involved enough to understand and detached enough to judge. This section explains what Mowles means by paradox, and how each tension has a failure at either end.

Complex responsive processes thinking, in Stacey’s and Mowles’s hands, treats paradox not as a puzzle to be solved but as the ordinary condition of social life. A paradox in this strict sense is not a dilemma, where one chooses between two options, nor a contradiction to be eliminated, but a pair of opposed ideas held together, neither of which can be dropped.

The social paradox of forming and being formed is the root case; Mowles returns to it in “Resources on the complexity of social life” (August 2026). Others recur across the corpus: being in charge but not in control; detachment and involvement, which Mowles takes from Elias; stability and change, the subject of “Stability and change in the minute particulars”; the local and the global; speaking and listening in Burke’s parlour.

Paradox, dilemma and contradiction

It is worth separating three words that are often used loosely. A dilemma is a choice between two options where you must pick one: sell now or hold. A contradiction is a logical inconsistency that should be removed. A paradox, in the sense Mowles uses, is a pair of opposed ideas that are both true and both needed, so that dropping either one produces failure. A receiver must shape the appointment and be shaped by it; must stay steady and change course. The skill is not choosing between them but noticing, moment by moment, which one is being neglected.

Receivership sharpens every one of them. The receiver is appointed to take control, by deed, and finds that almost nothing on the parade can be controlled. The receiver must be detached enough to report to the lender and involved enough to know what is happening on the forecourt. The strategy must be stable enough for the committee to rely on and open enough to change when the café asks to surrender. Each paradox has two failures, one at each pole, and the failures are recognisable types: the textbook receiver who promises control, writes from nowhere and never changes course; and the facilitator who never decides, absorbed into the parade’s own story.

Two failure types

The two failure types in the instrument are recognisable characters. The textbook receiver leans to one pole of every paradox: control, detachment, holding steady, speaking. The facilitator who never decides leans to the other: letting go, involvement, constant change, listening. Each looks like a virtue from inside, and each fails in ways the other can see clearly. Most practitioners lean one way by temperament, and knowing which way is half the work.

13 · The cable

Chain or cable: Peirce, Foulkes and the valuation

In brief

A valuation is often built like a chain: one line of reasoning resting on a few assumptions. This section shows how a receiver can build a stronger case for value from many different accounts, like the fibres of a cable.

The valuation is where receivership most needs Peirce. A conventional valuation is a chain: market evidence, comparable sales, a yield, an assumption about possession, hence a value. It is as strong as its weakest link, and in a distressed parade the weakest link is usually an assumption nobody has tested, most often that vacant possession can be had. When that link breaks, the whole argument does.

An inside-out receiver also builds a cable. Each account is slender: the café owner’s sense of footfall, the pharmacist’s head office’s view of the lease, the council housing officer’s interest in the flats, a local investor’s story about the last parade sale, the director’s grievance, the neighbour’s complaints. None would carry the appointment alone. Together, if they are many and come from different groups, they carry more than the chain, and they do not all fail at once. Foulkes’s threshold gives a rule of thumb: more than eight voices, from enough groups to deviate from each other’s norms.

Gathering the fibres

What counts as a fibre? Anything that gives an independent account of how the property is used and valued: the café owner’s sense of footfall, the pharmacy’s view of its lease, the council’s interest in the flats, a local investor’s story about the last sale, even the director’s grievance, which tells you what the borrower thought the parade was worth. None of these would satisfy a valuation standard on its own. Together, and alongside the formal valuation, they show whether the formal valuation’s assumptions are likely to hold.

14 · Free association

Agenda or free association

In brief

Most case meetings follow an agenda, which keeps them efficient and keeps out the concerns that often decide the appointment. This section explains Mowles’s case for leaving some time without an agenda, and what tends to surface when you do.

In “Stability and change in the minute particulars” Mowles describes the free-association groups at the centre of the Hertfordshire doctoral programme as “a structure without structure” and “a method without method”: a training in critical reflexivity that builds a more mature group of more robust individuals by attending closely to experience. He opens the essay with Whitehead’s warning about the harm done by people narrowly devoted to a single good method, and he is clear that such groups are not therapy but continuous inquiry into the task.

What free association is, and is not

The phrase can sound alarming in a professional setting. It does not mean a meeting without purpose, and it is not therapy. It means leaving part of the time without fixed items, so that people can raise what is on their minds and follow where the conversation goes. In Mowles’s doctoral programme these sessions are a training in noticing: what comes up, who raises it, what is avoided, how the group handles disagreement. The purpose remains the work; the method is to let the work show itself.

In “Staying with the trouble” (October 2026) he contrasts two events he attended: a research conference with shared meals, one keynote and open conversation about how the group was talking, often conflictual and for many the highlight of their year; and a facilitated workshop of timed exercises, flip charts and sticky-note voting, which was efficient and at which he got to know no one.

He borrows his late colleague Douglas Griffin’s phrase for the assumption that meaningful talk needs full structure: the doubling of process, a process for having a process. And he answers Mike Jackson’s criticism that conversation-led approaches are like a badly chaired meeting: sometimes going nowhere is the point, because if our problems are second-order (social and political rather than technical) then how we currently handle them is itself data.

Why agendas exclude

An agenda is a list of what the organiser expects to matter. It is an outside-in instrument, and like any map it leaves things out. The things it leaves out are usually the ones nobody wants to put in writing: fear of blame, rivalry, the wish to keep an instruction, the receiver’s own wish to be liked. These concerns do not disappear because they are not on the agenda. They shape the meeting from below, and they often decide what happens after it.

Receivership case meetings are almost always agenda-driven: rent, repairs, valuation, marketing, fees, any other business. The agenda is necessary. It is also a reliable way to keep out the themes that will decide the appointment: the relationship manager’s fear of being blamed for the 2019 approval, the director’s grief, the agent’s fear of losing the instruction, the receiver’s own wish to be liked. Run the same hour both ways below and watch what rises above the line.

15 · Gossip

Gossip, hidden transcripts and thought collectives

In brief

What people say in a meeting is not always what they think; what they say afterwards, among trusted colleagues, is often closer. This section explains public and hidden transcripts, gossip as a form of power, and how a profession’s shared assumptions resist new ideas.

“DM me if you want to know who I’m really talking about” (May 2026) is Mowles’s essay on gossip, which he treats not as noise but as one of the routine ways people stabilise or undermine power relations, claim status and decide who belongs. It usually stays hidden; when it surfaces, by accident or design, it can threaten someone’s place in the group. He illustrates it with a misdirected chat message, and with a meeting he facilitated where staff said they were afraid to speak up, a woman finally described being bullied, and the director reacted visibly and said nothing. She left within weeks. A sympathetic response, he suggests, could have signalled real change.

Gossip as information about power

It is tempting to dismiss gossip as idle talk. Mowles takes it seriously because it does real work. It tells people who is in and who is out, whose reputation is rising and whose is falling, what is acceptable and what is not. It is usually hidden from those it concerns, which is why its effects can seem mysterious to them. For a receiver, the gossip on a parade is information: not about whether a story is true, but about who is telling it, to whom, and what it does for them.

He draws on three bodies of work. James C. Scott’s Domination and the Arts of Resistance (1990) distinguishes the public transcript, what people say openly in the presence of power, from the hidden transcript, what they say among trusted peers; Scott treats “speaking truth to power” as largely a myth, because the powerful usually know the truth already, and resistance succeeds only with shared understanding, sustained solidarity, limited repression and luck.

Norbert Elias and John Scotson’s The Established and the Outsiders shows established groups praising themselves through gossip into a “heroic we” while denigrating outsiders. And Ludwik Fleck’s Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact (1935) describes knowledge as produced by thought collectives with their own style of thinking, which resist new ideas in five stages: unthinkable, unseen, kept quiet, explained away, and finally kept alive by magical theorising.

Two transcripts

James C. Scott’s distinction is simple once stated. The public transcript is what people say openly when the powerful are present: polite, agreeable, careful. The hidden transcript is what they say among themselves: frank, critical, often funny. Both are real. The mistake is to take the public transcript as the whole story. A tenants’ meeting that ‘went well’ may simply mean that the hidden transcript was kept for the car park. Scott’s further point, that the powerful usually know the truth already, means that the issue is often not discovering what people think but making it possible to say it together.

A receiver is the outsider in the established figuration of a parade, and the established story about the receiver arrives before the receiver does. The receiver also belongs to a thought collective, of lenders, receivers, valuers and investors, which “knows” that receivership parades are sold. “The Things We Never Say” (June 2026) adds why such things persist: drawing on Bourdieu, Mowles notes that participants repeatedly said everyone knows what is wrong, yet everyone is invested in the existing game, and challenging it risks embarrassment, shame and scapegoating.

Thought collectives in receivership

Ludwik Fleck’s idea applies to professionals as much as to scientists. Every profession has a shared style of thinking: things its members take for granted without noticing. Among lenders, receivers and valuers, one such assumption is that receivership properties are sold, and sold soon. Fleck’s five stages describe how such a group resists evidence that does not fit: at first it cannot be thought, then it is not seen, then those who see it keep quiet, then it is explained away, and finally elaborate theories keep the old belief alive. Recognising the stage tells a receiver what kind of argument is worth making.

Why this matters for a receiver

Assume that the conversation after the meeting is the real one, find ways to hear it, and when an idea meets resistance, ask which of Fleck’s stages it is in before arguing harder.

16 · Uncertainty

The politics of uncertainty

In brief

Complex situations are uncertain, but the uncertainty is not shared equally. This section explains how the more powerful pass it down to those with less power, and how a receiver can choose to hold some of it instead.

Complexity is usually presented as a fact about the world: outcomes are uncertain and unpredictable. In “The politics of uncertainty” (September 2025) Mowles insists on asking who carries that uncertainty. Drawing on Peter Marris’s The Politics of Uncertainty (1996), he argues that uncertainty is not shared evenly. The more powerful can pass it down a hierarchy; those with least power keep most of what reaches them, and often feel most responsible for circumstances they cannot control. Dominant groups keep their position partly by narrating their choices as inevitable. His examples are frontline health staff who feel unable to take sick leave, and the concentration of pandemic deaths in deprived communities.

How uncertainty travels

It helps to follow uncertainty as if it were a physical load. A credit committee faces uncertainty about losses. It can hold that uncertainty itself, or turn it into a deadline for the relationship manager. She can hold it, or turn it into a fixed fee and a timetable for the receiver. The receiver can hold it, or turn it into a fee cut and service levels for the agent, who can turn it into slower repairs. At the bottom, tenants and residents cannot pass it on to anyone. They live with a dark stairwell and an uncertain future, and often blame themselves.

The same essay makes a second point that matters to every workout. Organisations run with ruthless efficiency, stripped of spare capacity, cannot absorb shocks and cannot innovate, because novelty depends on experiments, and most experiments fail. Hence his subtitle: novelty requires redundancy, not efficiency. He is careful to add that this is no excuse for waste.

Why slack is not waste

The word redundancy can sound like inefficiency. Mowles means something different: spare capacity that allows an organisation to absorb shocks and try new things. A receivership budget with a small contingency can pay for a second valuation, a meeting with the council or a repair that changes how tenants see the appointment. A budget with none can only do what was planned. Mowles is clear that this is not an excuse for waste; it is an argument that some apparent waste is the price of being able to respond.

A receivership is a cascade of uncertainty. The credit committee faces a provisioning decision; it passes timetable pressure to the relationship manager, who passes a fixed fee and a deadline to the receiver, who can pass a cut fee and tighter service levels to the agent, who can pass slower repairs to the contractors and the residents.

At each level the more powerful can push uncertainty down. A receiver can choose to absorb some of it: to hold a contingency in the budget, to give the agent room to experiment, to refuse a timetable that only works if the tenants carry the risk. Why this matters for a receiver

Before agreeing a tight timetable or a fixed fee, ask who will absorb the slack it removes. A receiver can absorb some uncertainty, within capacity; beyond capacity, the receiver becomes the point of failure.

17 · Metrics

Hitting the target, missing the point

In brief

Measures are meant to show how well something is going. Mowles argues that they also change what people do, often in ways that undermine the very performance they measure. This section explains why, and what ‘good enough for now’ offers instead.

Three of Mowles’s posts take on measurement. “Improving performance” (September 2025) begins with drama students learning to make their scene partners look good, and calls that group-mindedness: performance as a shared achievement. Against it stands the reflex of contemporary management to reach for key performance indicators, expected to rise for ever. Measures, he argues, are performative: they produce performances (looking busy, gaming the numbers, appearing to take the indicators seriously).

They act as disciplinary power and as a statement of values without an overt values statement; people discipline themselves in advance. He grants the moral case for measurement, as a check on professionals’ claims, and then shows how individual indicators erode the social conditions that performance depends on, with the example of a surgeon whose skill depends on an ensemble of colleagues, equipment and training. Being good enough for now, he concludes, can be liberating.

Why measures shape behaviour

The idea of performativity is clearer with an example. Suppose an agent is judged on rent collected. The measure seems neutral: rent is either collected or not. But the agent now has a reason to press hardest where pressure works fastest, which is usually on the tenants least able to resist, and to neglect anything that does not show in the figure, such as repairs or relationships. The measure has not just recorded the agent’s performance; it has produced a particular kind of performance. Mowles is not against measurement. He is against forgetting that measurement acts on what it measures.

“Delivering change” (August 2025) traces the language of delivery to the Prime Minister’s Delivery Unit and Michael Barber’s “deliverology”, in which social aims are turned into measurable indicators and programmes. Targets work largely because people make them work, by legitimate and illegitimate means; more targets are added to correct the distortions, until governance is nearly unmanageable. With Whitehead he calls the reduction of a process to a deliverable “misplaced concreteness”; with Elias, “process reduction”.

There is, he says, no thing to be delivered. “Playing the ratings game” (September 2025) extends the argument to the star ratings that now follow every transaction, drawing on Onora O’Neill’s Reith Lectures to argue that more data does not produce trust, and describing a four out of five that his solicitor’s firm treated as a failure as a kind of moral injury.

Delivery and misplaced concreteness

The language of ‘delivering change’ deserves a closer look. To deliver something, there must be a thing to hand over. But a change in how tenants, an agent and a lender work together is not a thing; it is a pattern of activity that has to be kept going. Whitehead called the habit of treating processes as things ‘misplaced concreteness’. In receivership, it shows up when a report says an exit was ‘delivered’, as if the parade, its tenants and its problems stopped at completion.

Receivership reporting runs on such indicators: rent collected, days to exit, cost ratio, reports on time. Each is reasonable. Each, pressed hard enough, produces its own performance: rent collected by pressing the most vulnerable tenants, reports on time because they say less, days to exit cut by selling into a weak market. 18 · Second-order problems

Second-order problems: the seeds of future difficulties

In brief

Many problems on an appointment are the side-effects of earlier solutions. This section explains the difference between first-order problems, which can be fixed within our current way of thinking, and second-order problems, which require us to change how we think about them.

“Stuck in the middle with you” (April 2026) sets out what Mowles calls a pragmatic theory of change, built on Rahel Jaeggi’s Progress and Regression (2025). Jaeggi distinguishes first-order problems, which can be solved within the way we currently understand a situation, from second-order problems, which arise from earlier solutions and from the way we understand the situation in the first place.

They cannot be fixed by more of the same. Progress, on this account, is not movement towards a fixed goal but movement away from the problems we have, through a reflexive enlargement of experience; the criteria for doing better emerge from within the experience itself. “Starting with experience” compresses the same insight into a sentence that every receiver will recognise: the seeds of future difficulties are contained in today’s solutions.

First-order and second-order, simply

A first-order problem is one we know how to solve: a broken stair light, a lease that needs renewing. A second-order problem is one that our usual solutions keep producing. If arrears rise because the agent’s fee was cut and he stopped chasing small sums, then pressing harder on arrears treats the symptom while the cause continues. Solving a second-order problem means stepping back to ask how the problem arose, which usually means questioning a decision someone made earlier for good reasons.

Receivership is unusually rich in second-order problems because so much of what a receiver inherits is the residue of earlier fixes. The arrears on the parade are not simply a fact about tenants; they may be the result of a director who took rent in cash when the bank account was frozen, an agent whose fee was cut and stopped chasing small sums, a council housing payment still going to an old account.

Each first-order fix for the arrears (press harder, cut cost, sell quickly) solves something and produces the next problem, often a larger one. The fourth fix in the instrument starts differently, by asking what the arrears are a case of, which is to say, by examining how the problem has been understood.

Progress as moving away

Rahel Jaeggi’s idea of progress can feel strange at first. We usually think of progress as moving towards a goal. Jaeggi suggests it is more often moving away from a problem we can no longer live with, without knowing exactly where we will end up. The criteria for doing better are worked out along the way. For a receiver, this is a realistic description of most appointments: the aim is rarely a fixed exit value set on day one, but a series of moves away from the worst problems, with the exit becoming clearer as they are resolved.

The instrument grows four trees from one root problem. Step through the generations one fix at a time, or compare all four. The point is not that the reflective path has no problems: it ends with problems too. They are smaller, more ordinary and more shared, which is what movement away from a problem looks like.

Why this matters for a receiver

Before fixing a presenting problem, ask which earlier fix produced it. The answer often points to a better first move than the obvious one.

First-order problem
A problem solvable within current understanding.
Second-order problem
A problem produced by earlier solutions or by the way a situation is understood.

19 · Act and reflect

“But what should we do?” Acting and reflecting

In brief

Critique is easy; deciding what to do is hard. This section explains Mowles’s answer to the question ‘but what should we do?’, drawing on Dewey’s idea that we should reflect while acting, avoiding both rushing in and waiting.

The obvious objection to everything so far was put to Mowles by a colleague after a workshop, and he wrote it up in “It’s all very well being critical, but what should we do?” (August 2025). A complexity perspective is very good at taking apart the dominant discourse of management, the colleague said, but appears to offer nothing in its place. Mowles answers with three thinkers. Gottfried Keller, quoted in Adorno’s The Jargon of Authenticity, on tearing things down to let in light and air: critique clears space.

Hannah Arendt, on thinking as an inner dialogue that unravels settled certainties like Penelope at her loom, and yet sharpens judgement, best done “without a bannister”. And John Dewey, in Art as Experience (1934), on the failure of rushing into action and beating down resistance with old solutions, and the equal failure of passivity: the ideal is to act and to reflect on the action, in its context, while acting. The practical form of all three is a question to ask in a group: what does this new situation require of us?

Why critique comes first

There is a common complaint about complexity thinking: it is good at showing what is wrong with plans, and poor at offering alternatives. Mowles takes the complaint seriously. His answer is that critique has a job of its own: clearing away assumptions that prevent people from seeing the situation as it is. Only then can a group work out what the situation actually requires. The alternative is not a different plan handed down, but a better conversation about what to do next.

For a receiver the objection arrives in its sharpest form, because receivership is an office of action. The deed has been executed; the rent must be collected by the quarter day; the insurer wants confirmation by Friday. Nobody is going to wait while the receiver reflects. Dewey’s point is not that action should wait, but that there are two ways of failing at it.

One is to rush in, apply the solution that worked on the last parade, and treat every objection as resistance to be overcome. The other is to wait for the situation to become clear before acting, which in a receivership it never does. The first is the more common failure in property finance, because it looks like competence; the second is the more common failure in organisations that have read too much about complexity.

The instrument below makes the difference visible. The white circle is the situation, and it moves every week, jumping when something happens: the sublet comes to light, the council takes an interest, the café asks to surrender, a local investor appears. Four styles chase it. Rushing in reaches the place where the problem was in week 0 very quickly, and stays there. Waiting barely moves. Reflecting without acting re-examines everything every week and closes the gap slowly. Acting and reflecting moves steadily and re-aims every fortnight. Then set your own speed and reflection interval and see where you would have been.

Two failures, one practice

Dewey’s two failures are worth keeping in mind as a pair. Rushing in looks like competence: decisive action, a clear plan, resistance overcome. Its weakness is that it acts on the situation as it was first understood and does not notice when the situation changes. Waiting looks like prudence: gathering information, avoiding mistakes. Its weakness is that complex situations never become clear enough, so the waiting never ends. Dewey’s alternative is to act and to keep watching how the situation responds, adjusting as you go.

Mowles’s practical version of this is a question rather than a method, asked with others rather than alone: what does this new situation require of us? It is a good question for a case meeting at week four, when the strategy agreed at week one is still being reported as on track.

Why this matters for a receiver

Set a regular moment, perhaps fortnightly, to ask with others what the appointment now requires, and be willing to change the plan when the answer has changed.

Critique

Act and reflect

Re-aiming

20 · Healer or revealer

Healer or revealer

In brief

Receivers are appointed to fix things, and the role of the expert who heals a troubled situation is comfortable for everyone. This section explains Mowles’s argument that the healer’s role can create dependency, and describes an alternative: the revealer, who helps a group see its situation for itself.

“Healing or revealing?” (July 2026) turns the question on the consultant, and fits the receiver closely. Drawing on Stephen Grosz’s Love’s Labour, Mowles contrasts two positions. The healer points out contradictions, resolves difficulties and offers tools to make the group whole; the vocabulary of diagnosis and dysfunction makes the role seem trustworthy, relieves anxiety, and creates dependency while hiding the healer’s own blind spots. The revealer joins the group in an open inquiry, admits the limits of their own understanding, brings the difference of an outsider with only a temporary stake, and treats insight as belonging to the whole group.

Mowles prefers the second. He takes from Hartmut Rosa the idea of medio-passivity, from the Greek middle voice: an alert readiness, neither waiting nor directing. And he warns, with Grosz, that the urge to help can conceal a wish for control. The post’s subtitle is a line of Mead’s: there is no more distressing person to have about than one who is constantly seeking to assist everybody else.

A receiver is appointed as a healer. The lender wants the problem fixed; the tenants want reassurance; the agent wants instruction. The inside-out receiver does the necessary fixing (secures the site, places the insurance, collects the rent), and declines the larger role. The question shifts from “what is the answer for this parade?” to “what is going on here, and what does it require of me now?”

The healer’s hidden costs

The healer’s role is attractive for good reasons. A frightened committee wants reassurance; an agent wants clear instructions; a borrower wants someone to blame or rely on. A receiver who provides all this is valued. The cost is slow and easy to miss. Each act of healing puts a little more of the outcome on the healer’s shoulders and takes a little more thinking away from the group. After a while, the group cannot function without the healer, and the healer’s own blind spots go unchallenged because nobody else is thinking about the problem.

The distinction is not a moral one between good and bad receivers. Healers are often excellent at the necessary work, and a frightened committee may need someone to hold its anxiety before it can think at all; Mowles draws on Bion’s idea of containment for exactly this. The difficulty is cumulative. Each act of healing relieves the room and leaves the receiver holding a little more of the outcome. After two months of it, the relationship manager cannot answer the committee’s questions without the receiver; the agent waits for instruction; the receiver’s own assumptions have gone unexamined because nobody else was invited to examine them. The revealer’s path is uncomfortable earlier and steadier later.

What the revealer does differently

The revealer is not someone who refuses to help. The revealer does the necessary work (secures the site, collects the rent, places the insurance) and declines the larger role of having all the answers. Instead, they ask questions that help the group see what is happening, admit what they themselves do not understand, and treat any insight as belonging to everyone. Mowles adds a caution from Bion: a group that is too anxious cannot think at all, so sometimes the revealer must first contain some of that anxiety before asking the hard question.

The instrument plays eight moments from the first two months of case meetings: the relationship manager asking for a value and a date, the agent calling the tenants difficult, a threatened challenge, a convenient valuation, a stair light raised as any other business, a request for a one-page strategy, the committee asking whether the receiver is in control, and the exit. At each you can heal or reveal. Four lines record the group’s anxiety, its dependency on you, its own insight, and your blind spot. A preset called Medio-passive reveals by default and contains when the room is too anxious to think.

Why this matters for a receiver

Notice when you feel relieved that the lender cannot manage without you. That relief is a sign the healer’s role has taken hold, and a cue to give some of the thinking back.

21 · Courage

Courage, drift and the small compromise

In brief

Courage in professional life is rarely a single heroic stand. Mowles argues it is lost, and kept, in small daily choices. This section explains how small compromises accumulate until going along becomes part of who we are, and what helps people keep noticing.

Declining the healer’s role takes courage, of an unheroic kind. “Thinking about the practice of courage” (March 2026) sets the leadership literature’s heroic courage aside in favour of something quieter, routine, distributed and social. Mowles draws on Manfred Kets de Vries’s account of sycophancy: anxiety overwhelms judgement, people fear exclusion, and through ordinary incremental compromises “adaptation becomes identity”. He draws on Bion’s “quiet daily labour of containment”, and on Arendt’s insistence that thinking is an ordinary faculty and that refusing to go along requires sustained reflection.

For a receiver the test is small and frequent: the first time a report to the committee is softened to make it easier to read, the first valuation passed on without asking what the valuer was told, the first time silence is chosen because speaking would cost the next instruction. “Noticing and talking about power” (October 2025) is honest about the costs: critique makes the critic visible; organisations that prize positivity or alignment may punish it; and there is always the demand for a “practical alternative”, which can be a way of shutting critique down. Mowles’s stance, after Clifford Geertz and Norbert Elias, is detached involvement: caring enough to observe critically.

Why small compromises matter most

Large ethical tests are rare and obvious. Small ones are frequent and easy to justify. Dropping one word from a report, leaving out one complaint, accepting one valuation without asking questions: each can be defended on its own. The danger is cumulative. Each compromise makes the next one easier and makes speaking up more conspicuous, because speaking now would draw attention to all the times one did not. That is why the most important moment to speak is often the first small one.

What makes Kets de Vries’s phrase so exact for professional advisers is the word adaptation. Nobody decides to become a receiver who writes what the lender wants to read. It happens by small, reasonable steps, each of which has a good justification: the word “uncertain” would alarm the committee; the stair light is being fixed anyway; the cash payments complicate the arrears figure; the joke about the nurses’ flat was harmless. The cost of speaking rises each time one goes along, because each silence makes the next remark more conspicuous. And at some point the compromises stop registering as choices. That is the moment the phrase describes: not a lapse, but a change in who one is.

What helps

Mowles draws on Bion to suggest that courage is social rather than solitary. People keep their judgement when they have somewhere to take their doubts: a colleague who will listen to the hidden transcript, a supervisor who asks hard questions, a peer group that notices drift. Arendt adds that thinking itself is a kind of resistance, because it unpicks the certainties that make going along feel natural. Neither is heroic. Both are ordinary practices that can be built into working life.

The instrument sets ten such moments over ten weeks. Go along or speak at each. Watch how far you drift from where you started, the felt cost of speaking next time, your standing with the lender, and how much you still notice. When noticing falls below the line, later moments are presented as if they were not choices at all, which is how they feel. Run the presets and compare them: speaking early is cheaper than speaking late, and speaking only while you still notice is a strategy that eventually runs out.

Why this matters for a receiver

Keep a short list of the things you agreed to leave out of reports, and read it monthly. It is the simplest way to see drift before it becomes identity.

22 · Noticing

Reflexive noticing: your own part in the pattern

In brief

It is easier to see patterns in other people than in ourselves. This section explains Mowles’s idea of reflexivity: noticing how we take part in the very patterns we criticise.

Reflexivity runs through every post in the corpus, and Mowles is precise about what he means by it. In “The Things We Never Say” (June 2026), writing about the Towards Relational Public Services conference, he calls difficult conversations a practice, and says that practice requires critical reflexivity: noticing how one has participated in the dynamic one now criticises.

One of the cases he describes, from a mental-health trust, found that the hardest part of improving collaboration was identity work, including recognising how each team, and oneself, had contributed to the unhelpful dynamic. In “Talking ’bout my generation” (August 2026) he observes a group of young people discussing power and sexism and reproducing, in the room, the patterns they were discussing; his advice to facilitators is to track who speaks and who stays quiet.

Reflexivity in plain terms

Reflexivity means turning attention back on oneself. In Mowles’s sense it is not private soul-searching but a practical habit: after a meeting, asking not only what others did but what part one played. Did I speak first again? Did I keep the peace when the disagreement mattered? Did I talk about the tenants rather than with them? These questions are uncomfortable because they reveal that the patterns we complain about need our participation to continue.

“Changing our mindset for organisational culture change” (April 2025) explains why this is so hard. Mindsets are not private switches but something closer to ideology: beliefs sustained by belonging to groups, with power, inclusion and exclusion built in. People rarely recognise their own, because identity is bound up with understanding, so change feels threatening, and resistance is often a form of resilience. Two social processes help: Foulkes’s “hall of mirrors”, in which others reflect us back to ourselves, and the risk of shame and exclusion, which can drive learning or close it down. The most we may manage, Mowles concludes, is to become “a connoisseur of our own imperfections”.

Why we cannot see ourselves alone

Mindsets, Mowles argues, are less like private beliefs and more like the shared assumptions of the groups we belong to. Because they feel like common sense, we cannot easily see them. Other people can. Foulkes’s image of a ‘hall of mirrors’ describes how others reflect us back to ourselves in a group, sometimes uncomfortably. The realistic aim, in Mowles’s phrase, is to become ‘a connoisseur of our own imperfections’: not to eliminate them, but to recognise them when they appear.

Most people mark the patterns other people produce. The most frequent pattern in this record belongs to the receiver.

The four Is: questioning the tools we use

In brief

Tools are never neutral; they carry assumptions about what matters. This section applies four questions Mowles proposed for artificial intelligence to the everyday tools of receivership: templates, valuations, dashboards and letters.

“Reflecting on what it means to be human” (August 2025) records a day in Sydney on artificial intelligence and management. Mowles does not argue for or against the technology. He proposes four questions to ask of it: whose interests it serves; what ideology it carries, what view of the world it presents as simply true; what it does to our identity, including our capacity to think for ourselves; and which institutions it will reshape. Against the promise of frictionless knowledge he sets embodied, resonant knowing, the kind that comes from being in a room with people, and he ends with hope rather than optimism.

The four questions, one by one

Each question opens a different angle. Interests asks who benefits from the tool and who is left out: a workout template serves the lender’s provisioning timetable before anything else. Ideology asks what picture of the world the tool presents as simply true: that an appointment is a set of boxes. Identity asks what using the tool does to the people who use it: whether receivers start writing for the template rather than about the property. Institutions asks what the tool will reshape over time: whether receivership becomes a reporting function. None of these makes the tool bad. They make it visible.

The four questions travel well beyond AI. A receiver works with tools that feel neutral precisely because they are everywhere: the lender’s workout template, the automated valuation, the KPI dashboard, the standard letter. “The problem with lenses” (September 2026) explains why that feeling of neutrality is the problem. Any device for seeing assumes a detached observer and an object already there to be seen, and the more familiar the device, the more its perspective is naturalised. The four Is are a way of making the device visible again.

26 · Duties

The duties don’t go away

In brief

Inside-out practice does not replace the law. A receiver has defined legal duties to the lender and the borrower. This section summarises the main principles and explains how inside-out practice fits within them.

An inside-out treatise for receivers would be irresponsible if it left the impression that conversation could replace the law. It cannot. A fixed charge receiver appointed under a mortgage deed and the Law of Property Act 1925 is, by statute, the agent of the mortgagor (s.109(2)), while appointed by, and owing his primary obligations to, the lender. That double position is itself a figuration in law: the receiver acts for one party, as the agent of another, and owes duties to both. What follows is a summary of the settled principles, not legal advice; the deed, the facts and current authority govern any real appointment.

In management. In Medforth v Blake [2000] Ch 86 the Court of Appeal held that a receiver managing mortgaged property owes a duty in equity to the mortgagor, and to others interested in the equity of redemption, to manage with due diligence. That duty is subject to the receiver’s primary duty, which is to try to bring about a position in which the secured debt is repaid. Earlier, in Downsview Nominees v First City Corporation [1993] AC 295, the Privy Council had described the receiver’s duties to the mortgagor as equitable, including good faith and the exercise of powers for their proper purpose.

The duties in everyday language

Put simply, a receiver’s first job is to try to get the lender repaid. While doing that, the receiver must act honestly and for proper purposes, and, when managing the property, must do so with reasonable care for the interests of the borrower and others with a stake in what is left after the debt. When selling, the receiver must take reasonable care to get a proper price at the time of sale, but can choose when to sell and need not improve the property first. These are summaries, not legal advice; the deed and current authority govern any real case.

On sale. In Cuckmere Brick Co v Mutual Finance [1971] Ch 949 the Court of Appeal held that a mortgagee exercising its power of sale must take reasonable care to obtain the true market value of the property at the time it chooses to sell. In Silven Properties v Royal Bank of Scotland [2003] EWCA Civ 1409 the Court of Appeal confirmed that the same principles apply to receivers, that there is no duty to improve the property or to increase its value before selling (for example by pursuing planning permission or lettings), and that the mortgagee and receiver may choose the timing of a sale.

Read inside out, these duties are not a constraint on the practice described in this treatise but its frame. Due diligence in management is, in practice, a matter of knowing what is happening on the property: who pays, who cannot, what is broken, who is talking to whom. The stair light, the cash route and the sublet are all things a diligent manager finds out by talking to people. The duty on sale to take reasonable care over price is better met by a cable than a chain: many accounts of the market, from many people, rather than one untested assumption about vacant possession.

And Silven cuts both ways. It means the receiver is not obliged to let and keep the parade, repair the flats or work with the council before selling. It also means that if the receiver chooses to, that is a choice, to be justified to the lender as serving the primary duty, recorded, and revisited. The danger the duties guard against is capture: a receiver who comes to share the tenants’ or the director’s story so fully that the lender’s position is neglected. Detached involvement, in Elias’s phrase, is the professional form of the duty.

How the two fit together

The fit between the law and inside-out practice is closer than it might seem. Managing with due diligence requires knowing what is happening on the property, which means talking to the people there. Obtaining a proper price is easier to defend when the evidence comes from many sources rather than one assumption. And because a receiver is not obliged to improve or hold a property, any decision to do so is a choice that must be justified to the lender and recorded. The main risk is capture: listening so closely to tenants or the borrower that the lender’s position is neglected.

Why this matters for a receiver

Record the reasons for significant choices in terms of the primary duty. It protects the receiver, and it keeps inside-out practice honest about whose interests it ultimately serves.

Your own appointment: a workbench

In brief

The workbench lets you map your own appointment: who is involved, who talks to whom, which groups are tightly knit. It then suggests where to start and shows how a hostile story might spread through your own network.

Every instrument so far has used the same composite parade. An inside-out treatise cannot finish there, because its method starts with the reader’s own experience. This workbench is for your own appointment, or the building you manage, or the portfolio on your desk.

What the analysis means

The workbench uses three simple ideas from network analysis. Degree is the number of ties a person has: high-degree people hear and pass on a lot. Betweenness measures how often someone sits on the shortest route between others: high-betweenness people, or brokers, connect groups that would otherwise not talk. Density measures how many of the possible ties within a group actually exist: dense groups are cohesive and act together. None of these numbers is a finding. They are prompts: who might I have overlooked, who could carry a message, which group will be hardest to reach from outside?

Add the parties: the lender side, the established occupiers who have been there longest and know each other, the outsiders, and yourself as receiver. Drag them into place. Click two parties to tie them together if they talk to each other; click them again to untie them. The workbench then reads the figuration you have drawn.

It finds the hub of each group (the person with most ties within it, marked with a dashed ring), the broker (the person who sits on the most shortest paths between others, often between groups), how cohesive each group is, and who nobody talks to. It suggests whom to speak to first. Run the story to watch a hostile account spread from whoever you choose as its origin, and see where your ties stop it.

Your map is kept in this browser, and you can export it as notes to take into the next case meeting, with four questions from this treatise attached. Three examples are provided to start from: the parade, a multi-let office block and a portfolio of tenanted flats. The analysis is simple network arithmetic: degree, betweenness and density. It is a prompt for noticing, not a finding. As Mowles would insist, the map you draw is already a story about the appointment, and it is your story.

28 · Dialogue

Inside-out and the Cynefin Field Kit: a dialogue

In brief

This treatise follows one built on Cynefin, and Mowles criticises frameworks of that kind. This section sets out, as fairly as possible, where the two traditions agree, where they differ, and how a receiver might use both.

This treatise follows one built entirely on Cynefin, and Mowles’s essay names “imagined estuaries” among the outside-in maps he criticises. It would be easy to stage that as a contest. It is more useful to set out where the two traditions meet and where they part, as fairly as possible, and to let a receiver use both.

Where they meet. Both reject the idea that complex social situations can be planned from outside and delivered. Both pay attention to emergence, to small moves with large consequences, and to the stories people tell. Snowden’s safe-to-fail probes and Mowles’s minor gestures are cousins; so are the estuarine framework’s preference for many micro-nudges over one large project and Mowles’s pragmatic theory of change. Both distrust alignment, best practice applied out of context, and the language of delivery. Snowden’s SenseMaker asks people to interpret their own stories, which is an inside-out move.

Why the disagreement is worth understanding

It would be easy to treat this as a quarrel between schools. It is more useful to see it as a disagreement about the role of tools in thinking. One side worries that frameworks quietly do our thinking for us, presenting one perspective as the way things are. The other worries that conversation without shared tools lets the loudest voices decide what is real. Both worries are justified. A receiver who understands both can use frameworks to start conversations and conversations to test frameworks.

Where they part. Snowden’s frameworks are, in his own terms, sense-making devices: they are meant to help a group talk, and he insists on contextualisation over categorisation. Mowles’s worry, in “Complexity and inside-out thinking” and “The problem with lenses”, is that any framework, however carefully built, carries the assumptions of a detached observer and a pre-existing territory, invites the observer to choose a view rather than argue for one, and risks naturalising a perspective, which he links to how ideology works.

In “Staying with the trouble” he takes on Mike Jackson’s critical systems thinking from the same position: structure is not always better than conversation, and going nowhere can be the point. A Cynefin practitioner might reply that a framework used well is precisely a way of starting a better conversation, and that conversation without any shared device can simply reproduce the power of whoever talks most. Mowles’s own posts give that reply some force: “Talking ’bout my generation” shows the room reproducing the very patterns it discussed.

What a receiver might take from both. Use a framework to talk with, not to think for you. Notice when a framework ends the conversation instead of starting it, and put it down. Prefer self-interpreted stories to someone else’s categories. Treat every map, including this treatise’s instruments, as last month’s map. And ask of each framework what Mowles asks of every lens: who chose it, what does it assume, and what is it unable to show?

“Reflecting on what it means to be human” (August 2025), from a day on AI and management in Sydney, offers a test that serves for both: the four Is of interests, ideology, identity and institutions. Whose interests does this framework serve? What ideology does it carry? What does relying on it do to our identity as practitioners and to our capacity to think? Which institutions will it reshape? Asked of a lender’s workout framework, or of a receiver’s favourite model, they are a good way to keep the conversation open.

Why this matters for a receiver

Use any framework to talk with, not to think for you, and put it down when it starts ending conversations instead of starting them.

29 · Objections

Objections and replies

In brief

A treatise that argues for humility owes its readers the strongest objections to its own case. This section sets out seven, with replies that try to concede what should be conceded.

A treatise that argues against detached certainty owes its readers the strongest objections to its own argument. Seven follow, put as a sceptical lender, an experienced receiver and a critical scholar might put them, with replies that try not to win too easily.

How to read the objections

The objections are written as practitioners and scholars might actually put them, and the replies do not claim to settle them. Several, especially the charge that inside-out claims cannot be tested, are serious, and the honest response is to describe how the practice can be disciplined rather than to declare the objection answered. Readers who find an objection more persuasive than its reply are reading the section as intended.

1. It is slow and expensive

Lenders pay receivers to act. An hour at the café is an hour not spent on the sale. Some inside-out work is slower at first, and some appointments do not justify it: a vacant site, a single-let warehouse with a covenant-strong tenant. But on multi-let, occupied property the costs of outside-in practice tend to arrive later and larger: the broken timetable, the wrong valuation, the surrender that a conversation would have prevented. The simulation dramatises this; it does not prove it. The fair answer is to time-box the open conversations, not abandon them, and to show the lender what they found.

2. The lender pays for outcomes, not conversations

The receiver’s primary duty is to the lender’s recovery. Agreed, and the treatise does not dispute it. The claim is narrower: that outcomes on occupied property are produced by people, and that knowing what they are doing is part of managing with due diligence. A conversation that changes no outcome is a cost; one that finds the cash route is recovery.

3. It is unfalsifiable

Any result can be explained after the fact as emergence. This is the strongest objection, and Mowles faces a version of it in his three dilemmas. Inside-out accounts are not predictions and cannot be tested as predictions. They can be tested as accounts: by other participants who were there, by other cases, by the cable of evidence Peirce described. The discipline is to write file notes that another receiver could argue with, and to say in advance what would change your mind.

4. It is just good practice, renamed

Good receivers have always visited tenants and distrusted convenient valuations. Often true. Naming the practice still matters. It lets it be noticed, taught to juniors, defended to a committee that asks why the receiver spent a morning on the forecourt, and distinguished from the habits that look like it but are not, such as the reassuring visit that promises a rent freeze.

5. It risks capture

A receiver who listens to the director and the tenants will start to act for them. A real risk, and the duties section says why it matters. The answer is not to stop listening but to keep the double position in view: detached involvement, the receiver’s own part noticed, the reasons for each choice recorded in terms of the primary duty.

6. The treatise contradicts itself

It criticises maps and models, then offers twenty-two instruments, each a map. Yes. The dialogue with the Cynefin Field Kit and the four Is section say as much. The instruments are rehearsals, not representations of any real appointment, and they are offered to be argued with. If they replace the conversations they rehearse, they have failed. A reader who closes the treatise and goes to the café has used it properly.

7. Conversation favours the articulate and the powerful

Free association and open meetings let whoever talks most set the agenda. Mowles’s own posts concede this: “Talking ’bout my generation” shows a room reproducing the patterns it discusses, and his advice is to track who speaks and who stays silent. For a receiver this means seeking out the people who will not come to the meeting (the nurses on nights, the sublet, the older resident) and noticing whose account fills the file.

Why this matters for a receiver

Bring one of these objections to your next discussion with a lender or a colleague. Arguing it through together is itself an inside-out practice.
